LETKF: the local ensemble transform Kalman filter

A global ETKF on a large grid has two problems, and chapter 6 ends on both. Its update is confined to the NE−1N_\mathcal{E} - 1NE​−1 directions of the forecast anomalies, and its sample covariance contains spurious long-range correlations that those directions then act on. The LETKF Hunt et al., 2007, which follows the local filter of Ott et al. (2004), answers both by solving a separate small ETKF at every grid point, using only the observations nearby.

Three things organise this chapter. The local problem is an ordinary ETKF with one change, a weighted observation precision. The weights are set by a taper and a hard cutoff whose conventions decide how the analysis behaves in space. And the weights have numerical consequences for gradients and float32, which are specific to how gaussx implements them.

Why localize¶

Two independent variables have a true correlation of zero, but their sample correlation over NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​ members has a standard deviation of about 1/NE−11 / \sqrt{N_\mathcal{E} - 1}1/NE​−1​. A global update acts on all such correlations as if they were real.

It prints 0.335 0.333. With ten members, a correlation of one third between unrelated sites is an ordinary draw.

What locality buys¶

In the global ETKF the increment X~⊤wˉ\tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \bar{\vect{w}}X~⊤wˉ is a single combination of the forecast anomalies, so it lies in their span. In (8.5) every component takes its own combination, so the increment as a field is no longer in that span. This is why localization fixes the rank problem, and not just the spurious correlations: each point needs the ensemble to resolve only its local errors. Program 8.5 measures that fraction.

The taper, the cutoff and the half-width¶

Three conventions meet in (8.1), and they do not line up the way the literature might suggest.

radius is the half-width of the taper, and also the hard cutoff. filterax defines its tapers by a half-width: gaspari_cohn(d, radius) Gaspari & Cohn, 1999 is piecewise rational in z=d/ℓz = d / \ellz=d/ℓ, with a fifth-order polynomial on z≤1z \le 1z≤1 and a branch with a −2/(3z)-2 / (3 z)−2/(3z) term on 1<z<21 < z < 21<z<2, and it is zero for every d≥2ℓd \ge 2 \elld≥2ℓ. LETKF then drops every observation beyond d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ by the explicit cutoff. So the second branch of the polynomial, on 1<z≤21 < z \le 21<z≤2, is never reached, and the default weight is only the first branch:

ρ(d;ℓ)=−14z5+12z4+58z3−53z2+1,z=dℓ≤1.\rho(d; \ell) = -\frac{1}{4} z^5 + \frac{1}{2} z^4 + \frac{5}{8} z^3 - \frac{5}{3} z^2 + 1, \qquad z = \frac{d}{\ell} \le 1.ρ(d;ℓ)=−41​z5+21​z4+85​z3−35​z2+1,z=ℓd​≤1.
(8.6)

The default weight jumps at the cutoff. At z=1z = 1z=1 this polynomial equals 5/245 / 245/24, not zero. So the weight falls from about 0.21 to exactly 0 as an observation crosses d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ, and with a strong observation that is a visible step in the analysis. A smooth transition from one grid point to the next is the usual argument for tapering in the LETKF, and it does not hold for the default.

The other uses of the same taper reach further. localization_matrix(a, b, r) of chapter 11 has no cutoff, so at one value of the radius its taper reaches 2r2 r2r where LETKF reaches rrr.

To make the weight continuous, pass a taper whose support ends at the cutoff. With half of radius as the half-width inside the taper, the weight reaches zero exactly at radius, and the cutoff then changes nothing.

The first five lines print 0.2083, 0.6065, 0.7358, 1.0000 and 0.0000, the weight at the cutoff for each taper in turn. The next prints 0.0165, which is what localization_matrix still gives at 1.5r1.5 r1.5r. Along the line, it prints 0.970 0.430 2.00 for the default and 0.970 0.131 1.40 for the full-support taper. Those are the largest increment, the largest change between neighbouring points 0.1 apart, and where that change occurs. With the default, 0.430 of an increment of at most 0.970 disappears between two neighbouring points, exactly at d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ. With the full-support taper, the largest step is the smooth slope of the taper, well inside the cutoff.

Which observations count¶

The taper_fn receives the distances and radius unchanged. So a Gaussian or SOAR taper is cut at d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ in the same way, at weights of about 0.61 and 0.74. Only the Layer 1 filter has this argument: the Layer 2 filterax.LETKF takes radius and always uses the default.

The distance is Euclidean in the coordinates given, and LETKF has no metric argument. A periodic or spherical domain needs coordinates in which the Euclidean distance means what you want. Those are points on a circle for a ring, and points on a sphere for the globe. Program 8.7 does this for a ring and compares it with plain coordinates.

Why the noise must be diagonal¶

LETKF raises NotImplementedError for anything but a bare lx.DiagonalLinearOperator, which also excludes a diagonal covariance wrapped in a tag or written as lx.IdentityLinearOperator. The reason for the limit is in (8.2).

Write SSS for the observations with non-zero weight. Removing the others properly leaves the precision ([Σε]SS)−1([\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon]_{SS})^{-1}([Σε​]SS​)−1, the inverse of the block. A zero weight instead leaves [Σε−1]SS[\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1}]_{SS}[Σε−1​]SS​, the block of the inverse, which is a different matrix unless Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​ is diagonal. So with correlated noise a weight of zero removes the observation’s residual and anomalies, and still changes how the others are read. The off-diagonal terms of the precision also scale with ρ\sqrt{\rho}ρ​ rather than ρ\rhoρ, as gaussx#787 records.

gaussx.etkf_transform itself accepts any noise operator with obs_weights. Program 8.3 compares a zero weight against dropping the observation, for both kinds of noise.

It prints 1e-17 2e-16 for diagonal noise and 3e-04 4e-03 for correlated noise, in the mean weights and then the transform. Decorrelating the observations first, as in chapter 7, makes the noise diagonal, although the whitened observations are no longer local to one site.

Cost¶

The implementation vmaps gaussx.etkf_transform over the grid points, and a vmapped function has one static shape. So the observations with zero weight are not removed: they are multiplied by zero and stay in the arrays. B-localization and R-localization¶

There are two places to suppress spurious correlations, and they are not the same operation.

Covariance, or B-localization (chapter 11), takes the Schur product of the sample covariances with a taper matrix. It acts in state space, it suits gain-based filters, and it comes through localized_kalman_gain.

R-localization, as here, leaves the sample covariance untouched and reweights the observation precision at each point. It acts in observation space, it keeps every local problem an ETKF in NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​ dimensions, and it suits transform filters, whose algebra never forms Σ^x\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_xΣ^x​.

Greybush et al. (2011) compare the two, and study how tighter localization damages dynamical balance. The practical rule is that a gain-based filter takes B-localization and a transform filter takes R-localization.

Algorithm¶

Program 8.4 writes one analysis out in dense numpy, dropping the observations beyond the cutoff instead of multiplying them by zero. That makes it an independent check on both the weights and the claim that a zero weight removes an observation.

Implementation in filterax¶

There are three layers.

  • gaussx.etkf_transform with obs_weights is one local solve, returning wˉi\bar{\vect{w}}_iwˉi​ and Wi\mat{W}_iWi​. Applying it with one row of weights per grid point is the local ETKF.

  • filterax.filters.LETKF is the Layer 1 analysis step. It takes radius and taper_fn, and it needs state_coords and obs_coords as keywords.

  • filterax.LETKF is the Layer 2 cycle of chapter 16, which takes radius and an inflator, and receives the coordinates at assimilate.

Differentiating through the weights¶

A local analysis is an ETKF, so it inherits the closed-form derivative of chapter 6, at repeated eigenvalues as well. The weights add three points of their own.

A weight of exactly zero has a derivative of zero, by convention. The factor Dρ1/2\mat{D}_\rho^{1/2}Dρ1/2​ has an infinite slope at ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0, so gaussx guards it and returns 0 (gaussx#787). For diagonal noise that is a convention and not the true answer, because the precision ρ/σ2\rho / \sigma^2ρ/σ2 is linear in ρ\rhoρ and a one-sided derivative exists. A compactly supported taper has no slope at its end, so differentiating through a taper that reaches zero is unaffected. Differentiating directly with respect to a weight sitting at zero is the case to avoid.

The derivative does not see the cutoff. With the default taper the weight jumps at d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ, and no gradient describes a jump. radius is also a static field, and passing it a tracer draws a warning from Equinox, so treat it as a hyperparameter and tune it by running.

A coordinate that coincides with an observation gives NaN. The distance is computed as a square root of a sum of squares, whose derivative is infinite at zero. So jax.grad with respect to obs_coords or state_coords returns NaN when a grid point sits on an observation, as in the usual observing network on a grid. Gradients with respect to anything else are unaffected.

It prints 1.667363 1.667363 for the noise gradient against its central difference, at the repeated eigenvalues of every local Λw,i\gmat{\Lambda}_{w,i}Λw,i​. It then prints 0.000000 0.068758 0.068758: the gradient at a weight of zero, the gradient at 10-12 and the forward difference. Finally it prints nan with the observations on the grid, and a finite 0.636013 when they are moved off it by 0.3.

Float32 with more observations than members¶

The route inside etkf_transform is chosen from the array shape, so it counts every observation, whatever its weight. With Dy≥NED_y \ge N_\mathcal{E}Dy​≥NE​, which is the normal situation for a global network, every grid point takes the ensemble-space route, even when only a few observations are within its reach. That route loses accuracy in float32 when the noise variances span many decades (gaussx#785, and chapter 6).

Program 8.9 builds one local problem with NE=5N_\mathcal{E} = 5NE​=5, Dy=8D_y = 8Dy​=8 and only two observations with non-zero weight, and solves it twice. The first solve carries the zero-weight observations and takes the ensemble-space route. The second drops them, which takes the rank-DyD_yDy​ route.

It prints 5e-03 with the zero-weight observations kept and 5e-08 with them dropped. So the accuracy limit comes from the global count, and LETKF has no way to shrink the arrays under jax.vmap. With ordinary variances there is no such problem, as the float32 run in Program 8.7 shows. With variances spanning many decades, use float64.

When it breaks¶

The LETKF removes the rank problem, and the local version of each ETKF limit remains.

  • A radius that is too small or too large. Program 8.7 measures both ends, and the right value depends on the ensemble size.

  • A step in the analysis. The default weight jumps at radius, as Program 8.2 measures. Use a taper whose support ends at the cutoff if smoothness matters.

  • A wrong distance. On a periodic or spherical domain, plain coordinates cut the domain at a seam. There is no metric argument, so embed the coordinates, although this twin shows little cost from the seam.

  • Lost spread. Local analyses still underestimate error, so a cycled run usually needs chapter 12.

  • Broken balance. Each point solves its own problem, so dynamical balances over scales longer than the radius can be disrupted Greybush et al., 2011. Use chapter 13 to look for this.

  • Non-local observations. An observation that integrates over a whole column or the whole domain has no single location, and obs_coords requires one.

  • Correlated noise. It is refused, as above. Decorrelate first, or use the batch filters of chapters 6 and 7.

When LETKF is the right answer¶

Use LETKF when Dx≫NED_x \gg N_\mathcal{E}Dx​≫NE​ on a state with meaningful coordinates and roughly local error correlations, and the noise is diagonal. That covers most geophysical grids, and the twin above is a small example of it. The solves are independent, so they suit an accelerator.

It is the wrong tool for a state with no spatial meaning, such as the global parameters of an inverse problem in chapter 9. For a small state with a large enough ensemble, the global filters of chapters 6 and 7 are simpler and cheaper.
