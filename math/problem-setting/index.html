The Bayesian filtering problem

Data assimilation estimates a system from a model of how it evolves and partial, noisy observations of it. The exact answer is a probability density over the state, and no large model can store or propagate that density. filterax replaces the density with an ensemble of samples, and updates those samples with a Kalman gain built from their own statistics.

Three steps organise this chapter. The Bayes filter states the exact recursion, and shows why it cannot be computed. Linear maps and Gaussian errors close the recursion on two moments, which is the Kalman filter, and ensembles estimate those moments by sampling. Finally, the same update serves two families of method, sequential filters for a state that moves and iterative processes for an unknown that stays still.

The state-space model¶

Let xt∈RDx\vect{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x}xt​∈RDx​ be the state at window ttt and yt∈RDy\vect{y}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}yt​∈RDy​ the observations at that window. Two equations define the problem:

xt=f(xt−1)+ηt,ηt∼N(0,Ση),\vect{x}_t = f(\vect{x}_{t-1}) + \gvect{\eta}_t, \qquad \gvect{\eta}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \gmat{\Sigma}_\eta),xt​=f(xt−1​)+ηt​,ηt​∼N(0,Ση​),
(1.1)
yt=h(xt)+εt,εt∼N(0,Σε).\vect{y}_t = h(\vect{x}_t) + \gvect{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \gvect{\varepsilon}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon).yt​=h(xt​)+εt​,εt​∼N(0,Σε​).
(1.2)

The dynamics f:RDx→RDxf : \mathbb{R}^{D_x} \to \mathbb{R}^{D_x}f:RDx​→RDx​ may be a discretised PDE, an ODE integrator or a learned emulator. The observation operator h:RDx→RDyh : \mathbb{R}^{D_x} \to \mathbb{R}^{D_y}h:RDx​→RDy​ maps the state to what an instrument measures (chapter 3). The covariances Ση∈RDx×Dx\gmat{\Sigma}_\eta \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_x}Ση​∈RDx​×Dx​ and Σε∈RDy×Dy\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_y}Σε​∈RDy​×Dy​ describe the model error and the observation error. Both errors are independent of each other and across time.

These assumptions give the two conditional independences the rest of the chapter uses. The state is Markov, so xt\vect{x}_txt​ depends on the past only through xt−1\vect{x}_{t-1}xt−1​. And yt\vect{y}_tyt​ depends on everything else only through xt\vect{x}_txt​.

The Bayes filter¶

The exact solution alternates two steps Särkkä, 2013. Given the analysis density at t−1t-1t−1, the forecast step pushes it through the dynamics:

p(xt∣y1:t−1)=∫p(xt∣xt−1) p(xt−1∣y1:t−1) dxt−1.p(\vect{x}_t \mid \vect{y}_{1:t-1}) = \int p(\vect{x}_t \mid \vect{x}_{t-1}) \, p(\vect{x}_{t-1} \mid \vect{y}_{1:t-1}) \, \mathrm{d}\vect{x}_{t-1}.p(xt​∣y1:t−1​)=∫p(xt​∣xt−1​)p(xt−1​∣y1:t−1​)dxt−1​.
(1.3)

The analysis step conditions on the new observation by Bayes’ theorem:

p(xt∣y1:t)=p(yt∣xt) p(xt∣y1:t−1)p(yt∣y1:t−1).p(\vect{x}_t \mid \vect{y}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\vect{y}_t \mid \vect{x}_t) \, p(\vect{x}_t \mid \vect{y}_{1:t-1})}{p(\vect{y}_t \mid \vect{y}_{1:t-1})}.p(xt​∣y1:t​)=p(yt​∣y1:t−1​)p(yt​∣xt​)p(xt​∣y1:t−1​)​.
(1.4)

The denominator is the evidence of this window, and it is worth keeping. Multiplying it across windows gives the marginal likelihood of the whole record:

log⁡p(y1:T)=∑t=1Tlog⁡p(yt∣y1:t−1).\log p(\vect{y}_{1:T}) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(\vect{y}_t \mid \vect{y}_{1:t-1}).logp(y1:T​)=t=1∑T​logp(yt​∣y1:t−1​).
(1.5)

That sum is what a filter can report about how well the model explains the data, and chapter 14 trains on it.

The recursion is exact and also out of reach. A density on a grid with 100 points per axis needs 100Dx100^{D_x}100Dx​ values, which is already 106 at Dx=3D_x = 3Dx​=3. Every practical filter approximates this recursion, and filters differ in what they keep.

Closing the recursion: the Kalman filter¶

Suppose fff and hhh are linear, f(x)=Fxf(\vect{x}) = \mat{F} \vect{x}f(x)=Fx and h(x)=Hxh(\vect{x}) = \mat{H} \vect{x}h(x)=Hx, and the initial density is Gaussian. Then every density in (1.3) and (1.4) is Gaussian, and the recursion closes on a mean and a covariance Kalman, 1960. The forecast step is

μx,t∣t−1=Fμx,t−1∣t−1,Σx,t∣t−1=FΣx,t−1∣t−1F⊤+Ση,\gvect{\mu}_{x,t \mid t-1} = \mat{F} \gvect{\mu}_{x,t-1 \mid t-1}, \qquad \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t \mid t-1} = \mat{F} \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t-1 \mid t-1} \mat{F}^\top + \gmat{\Sigma}_\eta,μx,t∣t−1​=Fμx,t−1∣t−1​,Σx,t∣t−1​=FΣx,t−1∣t−1​F⊤+Ση​,
(1.6)

and the analysis step is

μx,t∣t=μx,t∣t−1+Gtrt,Σx,t∣t=(I−GtH)Σx,t∣t−1,\gvect{\mu}_{x,t \mid t} = \gvect{\mu}_{x,t \mid t-1} + \mat{G}_t \vect{r}_t, \qquad \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t \mid t} = (\mat{I} - \mat{G}_t \mat{H}) \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t \mid t-1},μx,t∣t​=μx,t∣t−1​+Gt​rt​,Σx,t∣t​=(I−Gt​H)Σx,t∣t−1​,
(1.7)

with the residual rt=yt−Hμx,t∣t−1∈RDy\vect{r}_t = \vect{y}_t - \mat{H} \gvect{\mu}_{x,t \mid t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}rt​=yt​−Hμx,t∣t−1​∈RDy​, its covariance Σy,t=HΣx,t∣t−1H⊤+Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_{y,t} = \mat{H} \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t \mid t-1} \mat{H}^\top + \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣy,t​=HΣx,t∣t−1​H⊤+Σε​ and the gain Gt=Σx,t∣t−1H⊤Σy,t−1∈RDx×Dy\mat{G}_t = \gmat{\Sigma}_{x,t \mid t-1} \mat{H}^\top \gmat{\Sigma}_{y,t}^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_y}Gt​=Σx,t∣t−1​H⊤Σy,t−1​∈RDx​×Dy​. Chapter 4 derives the gain. The evidence of the window is N(rt;0,Σy,t)\mathcal{N}(\vect{r}_t; \vect{0}, \gmat{\Sigma}_{y,t})N(rt​;0,Σy,t​).

Two things stop this working at scale. The covariance has Dx2D_x^2Dx2​ entries, which is 1012 at Dx=106D_x = 10^6Dx​=106. And the forecast needs F\mat{F}F, a tangent-linear model and its adjoint, which a complex nonlinear fff may not have in code at all.

Ensembles: sampling the moments¶

The ensemble Kalman filter Evensen, 1994Evensen, 2003 replaces the pair (μx,Σx)(\gvect{\mu}_x, \gmat{\Sigma}_x)(μx​,Σx​) with NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​ samples, the rows of X∈RNE×Dx\mat{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_x}X∈RNE​×Dx​. The forecast sends each member through the full nonlinear model:

xt∣t−1(e)=f(xt−1∣t−1(e))+ηt(e),ηt(e)∼N(0,Ση).\vect{x}^{(e)}_{t \mid t-1} = f\big(\vect{x}^{(e)}_{t-1 \mid t-1}\big) + \gvect{\eta}^{(e)}_t, \qquad \gvect{\eta}^{(e)}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \gmat{\Sigma}_\eta).xt∣t−1(e)​=f(xt−1∣t−1(e)​)+ηt(e)​,ηt(e)​∼N(0,Ση​).
(1.8)

The sample mean and sample covariance of the result stand in for (1.6), and chapter 2 defines them. The analysis then applies a Kalman update built from those sample statistics, so that no F\mat{F}F, H\mat{H}H or adjoint is needed.

Two families¶

filterax covers two kinds of problem, with one protocol for each.

A sequential filter tracks a state that moves. Each window brings new observations, the filter assimilates them once, and the dynamics carry the result to the next window.

An iterative process estimates an unknown that stays still, θ∈RDθ\gvect{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta}θ∈RDθ​, from one fixed data vector, y=h(θ)+ε\vect{y} = h(\gvect{\theta}) + \gvect{\varepsilon}y=h(θ)+ε Iglesias et al., 2013. There is no time here, so the question is how to apply the data more than once without counting it twice. The answer is to split the likelihood into tempered factors,

p(θ∣y)∝p(θ)∏i=1Niterp(y∣θ)Δti,∑i=1NiterΔti=1,p(\gvect{\theta} \mid \vect{y}) \propto p(\gvect{\theta}) \prod_{i=1}^{N_\text{iter}} p(\vect{y} \mid \gvect{\theta})^{\Delta t_i}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N_\text{iter}} \Delta t_i = 1,p(θ∣y)∝p(θ)i=1∏Niter​​p(y∣θ)Δti​,i=1∑Niter​​Δti​=1,
(1.9)

where each factor is a Gaussian likelihood with the covariance Σε/Δti\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon / \Delta t_iΣε​/Δti​. Each iteration is therefore a Kalman update with inflated noise, with hhh evaluated afresh at the current members:

θ(e)←θ(e)+Σ^θy(Σ^h+Δti−1Σε)−1(y−h(θ(e))).\gvect{\theta}^{(e)} \leftarrow \gvect{\theta}^{(e)} + \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{\theta y} \big(\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h + \Delta t_i^{-1} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon\big)^{-1} \big(\vect{y} - h(\gvect{\theta}^{(e)})\big).θ(e)←θ(e)+Σ^θy​(Σ^h​+Δti−1​Σε​)−1(y−h(θ(e))).
(1.10)

Here Σ^θy∈RDθ×Dy\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{\theta y} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta \times D_y}Σ^θy​∈RDθ​×Dy​ is the sample cross-covariance between the members and their mapped values, and chapter 9 develops the family. Re-evaluating hhh is the point of iterating, because it replaces the single statistical linearisation of a filter with one at each step.

Agreement with the Kalman filter¶

With no model noise and a linear fff, the ensemble moments follow (1.6) exactly, because FX~⊤\mat{F} \tilde{\mat{X}}^\topFX~⊤ has the sample covariance FΣ^xF⊤\mat{F} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \mat{F}^\topFΣ^x​F⊤. The ETKF analysis matches the Kalman update on the sample covariance (chapter 6). By induction, the ensemble mean and sample covariance must then equal a Kalman filter started from the sample moments of initial, at every window.

It prints 1e-16 8e-17 7e-16, which is float64 round-off in the mean, the covariance and the sum (1.5).

Model noise changes this, because then (1.8) only matches (1.6) in expectation. Program 1.4 adds Ση=0.05I\gmat{\Sigma}_\eta = 0.05 \mat{I}Ση​=0.05I to the same system, and measures the analysis mean against the Kalman mean over 20 independent runs.

It prints N_E = 20: RMSE 0.1345, x √N_E 0.601, N_E = 200: RMSE 0.0386, x √N_E 0.545 and N_E = 2000: RMSE 0.0146, x √N_E 0.652. The scaled column is nearly constant, which is the 1/NE1 / \sqrt{N_\mathcal{E}}1/NE​​ rate.

When it breaks¶

Two approximations separate an ensemble Kalman filter from the Bayes filter, and they fail differently.

The first is sampling. A small ensemble in a large state has spurious correlations and too little spread, and the filter can stop listening to the data. Chapter 2 measures the sampling error, and chapter 6 shows the resulting divergence on Lorenz-96. Localization (chapter 11) and inflation (chapter 12) exist to treat it.

The second is the Gaussian update, and more members do not fix it. Program 1.6 takes a two-component prior, p(x)=12N(x;−2,0.25)+12N(x;2,0.25)p(x) = \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{N}(x; -2, 0.25) + \frac{1}{2} \mathcal{N}(x; 2, 0.25)p(x)=21​N(x;−2,0.25)+21​N(x;2,0.25), with y=x+εy = x + \varepsilony=x+ε, y=0.5y = 0.5y=0.5 and unit noise variance. The exact posterior comes from quadrature on a grid, which is affordable in one dimension.

The answer settles quickly and stays wrong, in both the mean and the variance. The update sees only the first two moments of the prior, so it cannot know that the probability sits in two lobes. This is an error of the closure and not of sampling. Nonlinear observation operators fail in a related way, which chapter 3 measures.

When an ensemble Kalman method is the right answer¶

Use a sequential filter when the state is large, fff is expensive and has no adjoint, and the forecast density is roughly unimodal. Use an iterative process when the unknown is static and hhh is cheap enough to run many times, as in calibration.

Reach for something else in three cases. A small linear-Gaussian problem has an exact answer, in SquareRootKF and KalmanSmoother (chapter 17). A strongly multimodal density needs a method that does not close on two moments, and filterax does not offer one. And when fff does have an adjoint, a variational method is a real competitor.
