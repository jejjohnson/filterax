Numerics: where filters fail quietly

The algebra of a Kalman update fits on a page, and floating point is where it goes wrong. The failures of interest raise nothing: the arrays stay finite, the covariance stays positive definite, and the answer is wrong. A library that wants float32 and gradients has to design around them.

Seven themes recur across filterax and gaussx, and they organise this chapter. Each one gets a small demonstration, and the chapter ends with the limits that are still open. Throughout, ϵ\epsilonϵ is the machine epsilon, about 1.2×10−71.2 \times 10^{-7}1.2×10−7 in float32 and 2.2×10−162.2 \times 10^{-16}2.2×10−16 in float64.

Forming a Gram matrix squares the condition number¶

Take anomalies X~∈RNE×Dx\tilde{\mat{X}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_x}X~∈RNE​×Dx​ with the thin singular value decomposition X~=Udiag(σ)V⊤\tilde{\mat{X}} = \mat{U} \op{diag}(\gvect{\sigma}) \mat{V}^\topX~=Udiag(σ)V⊤, where σ∈RR\gvect{\sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^Rσ∈RR is non-negative and sorted down. The Gram matrix is then

X~X~⊤=Udiag(σ2)U⊤,κ(X~X~⊤)=κ(X~)2,\tilde{\mat{X}} \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top = \mat{U} \op{diag}(\gvect{\sigma}^2) \mat{U}^\top, \qquad \kappa(\tilde{\mat{X}} \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top) = \kappa(\tilde{\mat{X}})^2,X~X~⊤=Udiag(σ2)U⊤,κ(X~X~⊤)=κ(X~)2,
(18.1)

where κ\kappaκ is the ratio of the largest singular value to the smallest. An eigendecomposition resolves eigenvalues only to about ϵσ12\epsilon \sigma_1^2ϵσ12​. So a singular value below about ϵσ1\sqrt{\epsilon} \sigma_1ϵ​σ1​, which is 3.5×10−4σ13.5 \times 10^{-4} \sigma_13.5×10−4σ1​ in float32, is lost once the Gram matrix has been formed, however careful the solver. Working from X~\tilde{\mat{X}}X~ itself resolves it down to about ϵσ1\epsilon \sigma_1ϵσ1​.

Program 18.1 builds anomalies with σ=(1,10−1,…,10−4)\gvect{\sigma} = (1, 10^{-1}, \dots, 10^{-4})σ=(1,10−1,…,10−4) and reads the singular values both ways. It then takes one backward smoother step, where the smoothed state differs from the forecast along the strongest and the weakest mode. EnKS calls gaussx.ensemble_rts_step, which takes the pseudo-inverse from the singular values of the anomalies and differentiates it by the closed form of Golub & Pereyra (1973). EnsembleSqrtSmoother forms the Gram matrix instead.

In float32 EnKS misses the reference by 2.8e-04 of the size of the smoothed shift and EnsembleSqrtSmoother by 9.5e-02. The second drops the mode on purpose: its cutoff is NEϵσ12N_\mathcal{E} \epsilon \sigma_1^2NE​ϵσ12​ on the eigenvalues, which at NE=12N_\mathcal{E} = 12NE​=12 removes everything below about 1.2×10−3σ11.2 \times 10^{-3} \sigma_11.2×10−3σ1​. So in float32, prefer EnKS when a weak direction matters.

The ETKF applies the same idea to observations. Its rank-DyD_yDy​ route takes a thin QR of H~\tilde{\mat{H}}H~ rather than decomposing Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​, as in chapter 6.

Finite and positive definite do not mean right¶

It is tempting to test a float32 filter by asserting that its output is finite and its covariance positive definite. That check passes on broken code, because the covariance can be right while the mean beside it is not. The test that catches it compares means and gradients with a float64 reference, and that is what test_square_root_kf_float32_matches_a_float64_reference does.

Program 18.2 has two cases. The first is the ensemble-space route of the ETKF on noise variances spanning twelve decades, the setting of gaussx#785. The second is the mean shift of the parallel filter from chapter 17. With Σ=LL⊤\gmat{\Sigma} = \mat{L} \mat{L}^\topΣ=LL⊤ and Λ=LlikLlik⊤\gmat{\Lambda} = \mat{L}_\text{lik} \mat{L}_\text{lik}^\topΛ=Llik​Llik⊤​, both in RDx×Dx\mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_x}RDx​×Dx​, and b∈RDx\vect{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x}b∈RDx​, two forms of the same vector are

(I+ΣΛ)−1Σb=Σb−MM⊤ΛΣb=MM⊤b,MM⊤=(I+ΣΛ)−1Σ,(\mat{I} + \gmat{\Sigma} \gmat{\Lambda})^{-1} \gmat{\Sigma} \vect{b} = \gmat{\Sigma} \vect{b} - \mat{M} \mat{M}^\top \gmat{\Lambda} \gmat{\Sigma} \vect{b} = \mat{M} \mat{M}^\top \vect{b}, \qquad \mat{M} \mat{M}^\top = (\mat{I} + \gmat{\Sigma} \gmat{\Lambda})^{-1} \gmat{\Sigma},(I+ΣΛ)−1Σb=Σb−MM⊤ΛΣb=MM⊤b,MM⊤=(I+ΣΛ)−1Σ,
(18.2)

where M=LΞ−⊤\mat{M} = \mat{L} \gmat{\Xi}^{-\top}M=LΞ−⊤ comes from a triangular factor Ξ\gmat{\Xi}Ξ of a stack holding an identity, so it is well defined at any strength. The first form is the push-through identity, and it subtracts two large vectors whose difference is small. The second has nothing to cancel.

The first case prints True, 1.0e-03 1.000 and 0.06. Every entry is finite and the eigenvalues of W\mat{W}W lie in the range the theory allows, yet the mean weights are 6% wrong on this draw.

Gradients need the same test, against float64 and against a central difference. Program 18.3 differentiates the log-likelihood of SquareRootKF on a strongly observed model with Dy<DxD_y < D_xDy​<Dx​, where the likelihood factors are rank deficient. gaussx handles that with a custom derivative. With L=AQ\mat{L} = \mat{A} \mat{Q}L=AQ from a QR, the tangent is dL=dAQ\mathrm{d} \mat{L} = \mathrm{d} \mat{A} \mat{Q}dL=dAQ, which drops a term that only rotates L\mat{L}L and so never changes LL⊤\mat{L} \mat{L}^\topLL⊤. That needs no R−1\mat{R}^{-1}R−1, so it stays finite at low rank. The same block compares the Jacobian of the ETKF mean weights in the two precisions, at noise variance 10-8.

It prints 5.0288 5.0288 5.0245 and 4e-07. The float64 gradient matches the central difference, the float32 gradient is 0.09% away, and the Jacobian of wˉ\bar{\vect{w}}wˉ matches to float32 round-off.

A repeated eigenvalue and gap-free derivatives¶

Chapter 6 derives this in full, so here is only the point. Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ has the eigenvalue c=NE−1c = N_\mathcal{E} - 1c=NE​−1 repeated whenever Dy<NE−1D_y < N_\mathcal{E} - 1Dy​<NE​−1, and JAX’s rule for eigh divides by eigenvalue gaps. A spectral function needs no such division once its divided differences are written in closed form.

It prints 25.645979 25.645979, then nan -1.521080 -1.521080. The values agree, and the derivative through eigh is nan while the gap-free rule matches the central difference.

Eigenvalue floors¶

Every eigenvalue of Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ is at least ccc, exactly, because H~Σε−1H~⊤\tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\topH~Σε−1​H~⊤ is positive semi-definite. A float32 eigendecomposition does not know that. It resolves eigenvalues only to about ϵ\epsilonϵ times the largest, so a strong observation can return a computed γi\gamma_iγi​ far below ccc, or below zero. Then c/γi\sqrt{c / \gamma_i}c/γi​​ is NaN. That was gaussx#790, now closed: the ensemble-space route floors the computed eigenvalues at ccc.

The floor is safe because it is a bound the exact matrix satisfies, so it can only move an eigenvalue toward the truth. A floor that is not such a bound changes the answer without saying so.

Program 18.5 reproduces the setting of that issue, with Dy=30D_y = 30Dy​=30 observations of NE=8N_\mathcal{E} = 8NE​=8 members, where the ensemble-space route is the only one.

It prints:

Here c=7c = 7c=7, so the computed eigenvalue is 69 below its exact bound on the harder row. The floor fixes what it was meant to fix: nothing is non-finite. It does not make the answer right: on that row the mean weights are off by nearly four times their own size. That is relative to a float64 solve on the same inputs. A floor restores finiteness and not accuracy, and this is the limit gaussx#785 tracks.

Combine terms before evaluating them¶

A derivative written as a sum of terms can cancel just as a value can, and a custom derivative makes the choice of terms yours. Two patterns appear in gaussx.

The first is a divided difference, which is what the Daleckii-Krein rule of chapter 6 divides by. For ϕ(γ)=1/γ\phi(\gamma) = 1 / \gammaϕ(γ)=1/γ it reduces by hand, and the closed form has no difference left in it:

a−1−b−1a−b=−1ab.\frac{a^{-1} - b^{-1}}{a - b} = \frac{-1}{a b}.a−ba−1−b−1​=ab−1​.
(18.3)

The second is a sum of derivative terms that are large and opposite. Take the scalar mean weight wˉ=ρ/Λ\bar{w} = \rho / \Lambdawˉ=ρ/Λ with ρ=1/σ2\rho = 1 / \sigma^2ρ=1/σ2 and Λ=c+1/σ2\Lambda = c + 1 / \sigma^2Λ=c+1/σ2, all scalars, for a strong observation. The product rule gives two terms, each near 1/σ21 / \sigma^21/σ2, whose sum is near −c-c−c:

dwˉdσ2=−Λ−1dΛdσ2Λ−1ρ⏟≈1/σ2+Λ−1dρdσ2⏟≈−1/σ2=Λ−1dρdσ2(1−wˉ).\frac{\mathrm{d} \bar{w}}{\mathrm{d} \sigma^2} = \underbrace{-\Lambda^{-1} \frac{\mathrm{d} \Lambda}{\mathrm{d} \sigma^2} \Lambda^{-1} \rho}_{\approx 1 / \sigma^2} + \underbrace{\Lambda^{-1} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} \sigma^2}}_{\approx -1 / \sigma^2} = \Lambda^{-1} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} \sigma^2} (1 - \bar{w}).dσ2dwˉ​=≈1/σ2−Λ−1dσ2dΛ​Λ−1ρ​​+≈−1/σ2Λ−1dσ2dρ​​​=Λ−1dσ2dρ​(1−wˉ).
(18.4)

Joining them is not enough, since 1−wˉ1 - \bar{w}1−wˉ is still a difference of nearly equal numbers. The residual has to be reduced as well, and here it has a form with no subtraction:

1−wˉ=cσ21+cσ2.1 - \bar{w} = \frac{c \sigma^2}{1 + c \sigma^2}.1−wˉ=1+cσ2cσ2​.
(18.5)

gaussx does this in matrix form for the mean weights of the rank-DyD_yDy​ route. It combines the three terms of dwˉ\mathrm{d} \bar{\vect{w}}dwˉ before evaluating any of them, around an analysis residual that is computed by deflating the strong modes rather than by subtracting.

The divided difference prints -1.0007e-08 -9.9999e-09 at a gap of 10-1, and -7.4506e-09 -1.0000e-08 at a gap of 10-3, where the form as written is 25% wrong.

Scale invariance: normalise before fourth powers¶

The Ledoit-Wolf intensity Ledoit & Wolf, 2004 is a ratio of two fourth powers of the anomalies,

λ=min⁡(1,b2d2),d2=∥Σ^x−μI∥F2,b2=1NE2∑e∥x~(e)x~(e)⊤−Σ^x∥F2,\lambda = \min\left(1, \frac{b^2}{d^2}\right), \qquad d^2 = \Vert \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x - \mu \mat{I} \Vert_F^2, \qquad b^2 = \frac{1}{N_\mathcal{E}^2} \sum_e \Vert \tilde{\vect{x}}^{(e)} \tilde{\vect{x}}^{(e) \top} - \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \Vert_F^2,λ=min(1,d2b2​),d2=∥Σ^x​−μI∥F2​,b2=NE2​1​e∑​∥x~(e)x~(e)⊤−Σ^x​∥F2​,
(18.6)

with μ=tr(Σ^x)/Dx\mu = \op{tr}(\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x) / D_xμ=tr(Σ^x​)/Dx​. Rescaling the ensemble by sss scales both b2b^2b2 and d2d^2d2 by s4s^4s4, so λ\lambdaλ does not change. The floating-point range does not share that invariance. Float32 holds about 10±3810^{\pm 38}10±38, so a state of size 1012 overflows at the fourth power and one of size 10-12 underflows to zero. So gaussx.ledoit_wolf_shrinkage divides the anomalies by their largest entry first, under a stop_gradient, because λ\lambdaλ does not depend on that scale.

It prints λ\lambdaλ for the naive form and for filterax:

The rule is general: when a ratio is scale invariant, normalise before raising anything to a high power.

Infinity times zero: mask before you divide¶

Automatic differentiation multiplies slopes, so an infinite slope meeting a zero gives NaN even where the answer is well defined. Two patterns cause it.

The derivative of ρ\sqrt{\rho}ρ​ at ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 is infinite, and a guard after the root does not help. jnp.where still differentiates both branches, and the branch it discards contributes ∞×0\infty \times 0∞×0. The same goes for a division guarded after the fact. The cure is to mask the argument first, so the unselected branch evaluates at a harmless value.

This pattern recurs across the code. _root_weights in gaussx masks ρ\rhoρ before the root, for a zero observation weight. The Ledoit-Wolf code decides the clipped case before dividing, because d2=0d^2 = 0d2=0 would have an infinite slope. EKSStableScheduler floors ∥S∥F2\Vert \mat{S} \Vert_F^2∥S∥F2​ inside the root, so its gradient stays finite at zero. And KalmanSmoother replaces a masked datum with jnp.where rather than multiplying by the mask, since a NaN times zero is still NaN.

It prints inf nan 0.0, then nan 0.0, then 19.608067 19.608067. The bare root has an infinite slope, the guard after it gives nan, and the mask before it gives 0.0, and likewise for the division. The gradient through KalmanSmoother with one NaN entry and one fully missing time matches a central difference.

Known open limits¶

These four issues on gaussx are open as of 7 November 2026, checked with gh issue view.

  • gaussx#782: nonlinear_kalman_update with joseph=True returns a covariance near 1019 at a rank-deficient predicted covariance, and its gradient is NaN. filterax’s UKI passes joseph=False to gaussx.sigma_point_kalman_update.

  • gaussx#783: validate=True on the same function rejects some well-posed float32 updates and misses indefiniteness at small scale, because its threshold does not follow the scale of the rounding. filterax never passes validate=True.

  • gaussx#785: the ensemble-space route of etkf_transform loses the mean weights in float32 with noise variances spanning many decades, as measured above. With Dy≥NED_y \ge N_\mathcal{E}Dy​≥NE​ there is no other route.

  • gaussx#787: the derivative with respect to an observation weight of exactly zero is 0, where the one-sided derivative is not. The masking above makes it finite, and it does not make it the one-sided value. The issue notes that a compactly supported taper has zero slope at its support radius, so this reaches only a weight optimised directly.

Program 18.9 reproduces the first and the last.

It prints joseph=True: error 1.2e+19, gradient finite False, joseph=False: error 1.8e-15, gradient finite True and 0.0000 0.6409. The last pair is the gradient at a zero weight and a forward difference with a step of 10-6.

What to test¶

The themes above reduce to a short list for any new method.

  • Compare float32 with a float64 reference, on the means and on the gradients, not only on finiteness or definiteness.

  • Include low-rank inputs, noise variances spanning many decades, and a state of extreme scale.

  • Check a custom derivative against a central difference in float64, at a repeated eigenvalue and at any boundary where a mask or floor acts.
