Worked example: a small nonlinear inverse problem

The ensemble Kalman processes of chapter 9 solve inverse problems without gradients of the forward model. They are built on a Gaussian update, so on a nonlinear problem it matters what each one actually returns. This chapter runs them on a problem small enough to have a grid reference, and measures what each gets right and wrong.

Three questions organise it. What does each method return next to the true posterior on one nonlinear problem? Which of its errors are there even when the problem is linear, and can be worked out by hand? And what happens when the posterior has two modes, which no Gaussian update can represent?

The problem and its reference¶

The unknown θ∈RDθ\gvect{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta}θ∈RDθ​ with Dθ=2D_\theta = 2Dθ​=2 sets the amplitude and the decay rate of a curve, on a log scale so both stay positive. It is observed at Dy=5D_y = 5Dy​=5 times τj\tau_jτj​:

gj(θ)=exp⁡(θ1)exp⁡(−exp⁡(θ2)τj),y=g(θ)+ε,ε∼N(0,σ2I),g_j(\gvect{\theta}) = \exp(\theta_1) \exp\big(-\exp(\theta_2) \tau_j\big), \qquad \vect{y} = g(\gvect{\theta}) + \gvect{\varepsilon}, \qquad \gvect{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \sigma^2 \mat{I}),gj​(θ)=exp(θ1​)exp(−exp(θ2​)τj​),y=g(θ)+ε,ε∼N(0,σ2I),
(20.1)

with σ=0.4\sigma = 0.4σ=0.4 and θtrue=(0.5,−0.5)\gvect{\theta}_\text{true} = (0.5, -0.5)θtrue​=(0.5,−0.5). That noise is large against the curve, so the posterior is visibly non-Gaussian. The prior is θ∼N(0,I)\gvect{\theta} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \mat{I})θ∼N(0,I), which gives

log⁡p(θ∣y)=−12σ2∥y−g(θ)∥2−12∥θ∥2+const.\log p(\gvect{\theta} \mid \vect{y}) = -\frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \Vert \vect{y} - g(\gvect{\theta}) \Vert^2 - \frac{1}{2} \Vert \gvect{\theta} \Vert^2 + \text{const}.logp(θ∣y)=−2σ21​∥y−g(θ)∥2−21​∥θ∥2+const.
(20.2)

In two dimensions this needs no sampling, since Program 20.1 evaluates it on a grid of 801×801801 \times 801801×801 points. The same block defines the scores used below: the mean, the standard deviations, the correlation and the skewness of each marginal. It also scores the distance of the mean from the grid’s, in posterior standard deviations. The last score is KL(p∥q)\mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q)KL(p∥q), from the grid to a Gaussian qqq with the method’s mean and covariance.

It prints:

  • grid: mean [ 0.386 -0.827], sd [0.244 0.529], corr 0.567, off [0. 0.] sd, skew [-0.31 -0.32], KL 0.045

  • MAP [ 0.45 -0.77]

The posterior is skewed, so its mean and its mode differ, by about a quarter of a standard deviation in θ1\theta_1θ1​. The KL 0.045 is a floor: it is the distance to the Gaussian with the posterior’s own mean and covariance, the best any Gaussian method can do.

EKI, UKI and EKS on it¶

All three methods are Layer 2 models with a run method, and all start from the prior.

  • EKI Iglesias et al., 2013 moves an ensemble with a tempered Kalman update until the artificial time reaches 1, with steps from DataMisfitController Iglesias & Yang, 2021. It has no prior term, so the prior enters only through the first ensemble.

  • UKI Huang et al., 2022 keeps a mean and a covariance, and evaluates ggg at 2Dθ+12 D_\theta + 12Dθ​+1 sigma points in each step, with the same scheduler.

  • EKS Garbuno-Inigo et al., 2020 adds a prior drift and ensemble-scaled noise to the EKI update, so that its ensemble samples rather than collapses. We discard the first 100 of its 200 iterations and pool the rest.

TEKI Chada et al., 2020 is here too, as a check on the mode, because it treats the prior as data and targets the MAP. EKS is the only stochastic method, so it also runs from a second seed, and once with twice the members and target_stiffness = 0.03 for 1,500 iterations. Those two use the Layer 1 EKS_Process in a jax.lax.scan, because the Layer 2 loop is too slow for that many steps.

It prints, with each line’s iteration count, the rows of this table:

EKI stopped after 4 iterations, UKI after 3, and EKS ran to an artificial time of about 11.5. TEKI prints a mean of [ 0.446 -0.744], next to the grid’s MAP of [ 0.45 -0.77]. EKS’s skewness is [-0.19 -0.19], [0.03 0.06] and [ 0.01 -0.1 ] in the three runs, against the grid’s [-0.31 -0.32].

Each method is wrong in its own way.

  • EKI gets the mean closest, at very little cost, and its spread is not a posterior: the standard deviation of θ1\theta_1θ1​ is 0.353 against 0.244.

  • UKI is the cheapest by far, with the widest covariance, 40% to 50% too wide on both parameters, and it cannot represent skew.

  • EKS gets the standard deviations closest, within about 13%, and has the lowest KL of the three. Its mean of θ1\theta_1θ1​ sits at the mode rather than the posterior mean, a quarter to a third of a standard deviation off. Its skewness is too small, and it varies between runs.

The shift in θ1\theta_1θ1​ is not sampling noise or step size. The values 0.459, 0.443 and 0.458 all sit near the MAP of 0.45, including the run with more members and smaller steps. The update uses ensemble statistics in place of ∇g\nabla g∇g, which is exact only for a linear ggg Garbuno-Inigo et al., 2020. That is the likely source, though we have not isolated it.

Why EKI’s spread is not the posterior¶

A linear g(θ)=Hθg(\gvect{\theta}) = \mat{H} \gvect{\theta}g(θ)=Hθ separates errors of nonlinearity from errors of the method, because the posterior is then known in closed form. With A=H⊤Σε−1H∈RDθ×Dθ\mat{A} = \mat{H}^\top \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \mat{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta \times D_\theta}A=H⊤Σε−1​H∈RDθ​×Dθ​ and a prior of covariance Σ0\gmat{\Sigma}_0Σ0​, it has precision Σ0−1+A\gmat{\Sigma}_0^{-1} + \mat{A}Σ0−1​+A.

filterax.EKI is deterministic: every member sees the same y\vect{y}y, with no perturbed observations. One step of size Δt\Delta tΔt updates member eee by

θ(e)←θ(e)+Σ^θh(Σ^h+Δt−1Σε)−1(y−g(θ(e))).\gvect{\theta}^{(e)} \leftarrow \gvect{\theta}^{(e)} + \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{\theta h} (\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h + \Delta t^{-1} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon)^{-1} (\vect{y} - g(\gvect{\theta}^{(e)})).θ(e)←θ(e)+Σ^θh​(Σ^h​+Δt−1Σε​)−1(y−g(θ(e))).
(20.3)

That gives a mean and a covariance with different gains, which is the difference from a Kalman update. With a single step Δt=1\Delta t = 1Δt=1 the mean takes the exact Kalman update. The anomalies are multiplied by I−G^H\mat{I} - \hat{\mat{G}} \mat{H}I−G^H, so the covariance contracts twice as far as it should. In the limit of small steps the flow of (20.3) is, for a linear ggg,

dΣdt=−2ΣAΣ,dmdt=−ΣA(m−θls),\frac{\mathrm{d} \gmat{\Sigma}}{\mathrm{d} t} = -2 \gmat{\Sigma} \mat{A} \gmat{\Sigma}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d} \vect{m}}{\mathrm{d} t} = -\gmat{\Sigma} \mat{A} (\vect{m} - \gvect{\theta}_\text{ls}),dtdΣ​=−2ΣAΣ,dtdm​=−ΣA(m−θls​),
(20.4)

with m∈RDθ\vect{m} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_\theta}m∈RDθ​ the ensemble mean and θls\gvect{\theta}_\text{ls}θls​ the least-squares solution, and it integrates to

Σ(1)=(Σ0−1+2A)−1,m(1)=θls+(I+2Σ0A)−1/2(m0−θls).\gmat{\Sigma}(1) = (\gmat{\Sigma}_0^{-1} + 2 \mat{A})^{-1}, \qquad \vect{m}(1) = \gvect{\theta}_\text{ls} + (\mat{I} + 2 \gmat{\Sigma}_0 \mat{A})^{-1/2} (\vect{m}_0 - \gvect{\theta}_\text{ls}).Σ(1)=(Σ0−1​+2A)−1,m(1)=θls​+(I+2Σ0​A)−1/2(m0​−θls​).
(20.5)

You can check this by differentiating: Σ(t)A=(I+2tΣ0A)−1Σ0A\gmat{\Sigma}(t) \mat{A} = (\mat{I} + 2 t \gmat{\Sigma}_0 \mat{A})^{-1} \gmat{\Sigma}_0 \mat{A}Σ(t)A=(I+2tΣ0​A)−1Σ0​A commutes with I+2tΣ0A\mat{I} + 2 t \gmat{\Sigma}_0 \mat{A}I+2tΣ0​A. So at t=1t = 1t=1 the covariance has counted the data twice, and the mean is not the posterior mean either. Neither is a matter of ensemble size.

UKI differs in exactly this respect, since it updates the covariance with the Kalman formula. Each step adds ΔtA\Delta t \mat{A}ΔtA to its precision, and the steps sum to 1, so on a linear ggg it lands on the posterior.

So read an EKI ensemble as a point estimate with a convergence diagnostic, and never as a posterior, even on a linear model. Its spread was too wide on the nonlinear problem above and too narrow here, so it can err either way.

The same inversion through optax¶

The same processes are available as optax transforms from filterax.optax, which lets an inversion sit in an optax.chain or a training loop. The ensemble lives in the optimiser state, and params holds the running mean. Its grads argument is ignored. The transforms have fewer options than the Layer 2 models: flx.optax.eks takes no prior, so it targets the likelihood alone.

It prints optax EKI: mean [ 0.376 -0.812], sd [0.35 0.568], corr 0.599, off [0.04 0.03] sd, KL 0.161 after 4 updates. That is the Layer 2 answer up to the first ensemble, which differs because the transform seeds its own draw and recentres it on params.

When it breaks: a posterior with two modes¶

Take g(θ)=θ2g(\theta) = \theta^2g(θ)=θ2 with scalar θ\thetaθ, y=1y = 1y=1, noise variance 0.1 and a standard normal prior. The posterior has two modes near ±1\pm 1±1, and the sign of θ\thetaθ does not affect the data. Since ggg is even, its covariance with θ\thetaθ under any symmetric belief is zero, so the Kalman update has nothing to work with. Program 20.5 measures the mean, the standard deviation and the mass within 0.3 of the origin, where the true posterior has almost none.

It prints:

Every method reports a plausible mean and standard deviation, and that is what makes this case dangerous. UKI from the prior mean returns the prior exactly, since its sigma points are symmetric, and starting from 0.1 hardly moves it. EKI and EKS keep members near the origin, 21 and 25 times the true mass, because they fill in the valley between the modes. Nothing here reports failure, so if the forward model allows several modes, check for them separately.

When each is the right answer¶

Use EKI for a point estimate when the forward model is expensive and has no gradients, since four iterations of 100 members got closest to the mean here. Use TEKI when you want the MAP under a prior. Use UKI for a Gaussian summary at very low cost on a near-linear problem. It is exact when linear, and it needs only 2Dθ+12 D_\theta + 12Dθ​+1 runs in each step, which grows with DθD_\thetaDθ​. Use EKS when you want samples and can afford thousands of forward runs, accepting that its mean leaned toward the mode on the skewed posterior here. Use a grid or an MCMC sampler when the answer must be right, if the problem is small enough to allow it.
