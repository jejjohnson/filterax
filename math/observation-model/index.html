The observation model

The observation model connects the state a filter estimates to the data it actually sees. An ensemble handles this connection without a Jacobian, by mapping every member through the operator and reading off sample statistics. This chapter works out what those statistics mean, and how filterax represents the operator and the noise.

Three ideas organise it. A sample cross-covariance is a statistical linearisation of hhh, which averages the Jacobian over the ensemble. The observation-error covariance is always an operator, so its structure stays available to the linear algebra. And stochastic filters need perturbed observations, whose draws follow that structure and whose sampling noise can be stated exactly.

The measurement equation¶

Observations are a deterministic map of the state plus additive Gaussian noise:

y=h(x)+ε,ε∼N(0,Σε),\vect{y} = h(\vect{x}) + \gvect{\varepsilon}, \qquad \gvect{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon),y=h(x)+ε,ε∼N(0,Σε​),
(3.1)

with y∈RDy\vect{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}y∈RDy​, h:RDx→RDyh : \mathbb{R}^{D_x} \to \mathbb{R}^{D_y}h:RDx​→RDy​ and Σε∈RDy×Dy\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_y}Σε​∈RDy​×Dy​. The likelihood is therefore p(y∣x)=N(y;h(x),Σε)p(\vect{y} \mid \vect{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\vect{y}; h(\vect{x}), \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon)p(y∣x)=N(y;h(x),Σε​). The operator can be a selection of grid points, an interpolation stencil or a full radiative-transfer model, and H\mat{H}H denotes a linear one. Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​ covers more than instrument noise, since it also has to carry the error of representing the measured quantity with the model’s state Janjić et al., 2018.

The ensemble in observation space¶

Mapping each member gives h(e)=h(x(e))∈RDy\vect{h}^{(e)} = h(\vect{x}^{(e)}) \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}h(e)=h(x(e))∈RDy​, and these stack as rows just as the state members do. Their mean hˉ∈RDy\bar{\vect{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}hˉ∈RDy​ and anomalies H~∈RNE×Dy\tilde{\mat{H}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_y}H~∈RNE​×Dy​ follow chapter 2. For a nonlinear hhh the mean differs from the map of the mean, hˉ≠h(xˉ)\bar{\vect{h}} \ne h(\bar{\vect{x}})hˉ=h(xˉ), and filterax always uses hˉ\bar{\vect{h}}hˉ.

The Kalman update needs two products with H\mat{H}H, and the ensemble replaces both, with c=NE−1c = N_\mathcal{E} - 1c=NE​−1:

Σ^xy=1cX~⊤H~,Σ^h=1cH~⊤H~.\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy} = \frac{1}{c} \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}}, \qquad \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h = \frac{1}{c} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}}.Σ^xy​=c1​X~⊤H~,Σ^h​=c1​H~⊤H~.
(3.2)

For a linear operator, H~=X~H⊤\tilde{\mat{H}} = \tilde{\mat{X}} \mat{H}^\topH~=X~H⊤ holds identically, and so nothing is approximated:

Σ^xy=Σ^xH⊤,Σ^h=HΣ^xH⊤.\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy} = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \mat{H}^\top, \qquad \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h = \mat{H} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \mat{H}^\top.Σ^xy​=Σ^x​H⊤,Σ^h​=HΣ^x​H⊤.
(3.3)

It prints 0e+00 0e+00, because this H\mat{H}H only selects components and so the two sides are the same numbers.

Statistical linearisation¶

A nonlinear hhh has no H\mat{H}H, but the anomalies still define one. Regress the mapped anomalies on the state anomalies, h~(e)=H^x~(e)+δ(e)\tilde{\vect{h}}^{(e)} = \hat{\mat{H}} \tilde{\vect{x}}^{(e)} + \gvect{\delta}^{(e)}h~(e)=H^x~(e)+δ(e), with H^∈RDy×Dx\hat{\mat{H}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_x}H^∈RDy​×Dx​ chosen by least squares and δ(e)∈RDy\gvect{\delta}^{(e)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}δ(e)∈RDy​ the residual. The normal equations give

H^=Σ^xy⊤Σ^x+,Σ^xy=Σ^xH^⊤,\hat{\mat{H}} = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy}^\top \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x^{+}, \qquad \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy} = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \hat{\mat{H}}^\top,H^=Σ^xy⊤​Σ^x+​,Σ^xy​=Σ^x​H^⊤,
(3.4)

where +^{+}+ is the pseudo-inverse. So Σ^xy\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy}Σ^xy​ already plays the role of Σ^xH⊤\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \mat{H}^\topΣ^x​H⊤ in (3.3), and the filter never has to compute H^\hat{\mat{H}}H^.

What this linearisation means follows from Stein’s lemma Stein, 1981. For Gaussian x\vect{x}x with covariance Σx\gmat{\Sigma}_xΣx​, and in the limit of many members,

Σxy=Σx E[Jh(x)]⊤,\gmat{\Sigma}_{xy} = \gmat{\Sigma}_x \, \mathbb{E}[\mat{J}_h(\vect{x})]^\top,Σxy​=Σx​E[Jh​(x)]⊤,
(3.5)

with Jh=∂h/∂x∈RDy×Dx\mat{J}_h = \partial h / \partial \vect{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_x}Jh​=∂h/∂x∈RDy​×Dx​. The regression coefficient is therefore the Jacobian averaged over the forecast density. An extended Kalman filter would use the Jacobian at the mean, which is a different matrix whenever hhh curves across the ensemble.

The residual is uncorrelated with the state anomalies by construction, so the mapped covariance splits in two:

Σ^h=H^Σ^xH^⊤+Σ^δ.\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h = \hat{\mat{H}} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x \hat{\mat{H}}^\top + \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_\delta.Σ^h​=H^Σ^x​H^⊤+Σ^δ​.
(3.6)

The part of hhh that no line explains, Σ^δ\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_\deltaΣ^δ​, enters Σ^y=Σ^h+Σε\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_y = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h + \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣ^y​=Σ^h​+Σε​ exactly like extra observation noise. Nonlinearity therefore reduces the gain, which is the right instinct as long as the line is a fair description of hhh.

Program 3.2 checks all of this with h(x)=tanh⁡(Hx)h(\vect{x}) = \tanh(\mat{H} \vect{x})h(x)=tanh(Hx), on 4000 members with mean (0.5,−0.2,1)(0.5, -0.2, 1)(0.5,−0.2,1) and standard deviation 0.6.

It prints 0.689 0.689 0.802, 0.462 0.470 0.420, [0.357 0.66 ] [0.445 0.761] and [0.1831 0.0946] [0.1723 0.0794]. The regression sits next to the averaged Jacobian and away from the Jacobian at the mean, as (3.5) says. The third line shows that hˉ\bar{\vect{h}}hˉ and h(xˉ)h(\bar{\vect{x}})h(xˉ) differ. The last line is (3.6): the diagonal of Σ^h\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_hΣ^h​ exceeds the explained part, and the difference is Σ^δ\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_\deltaΣ^δ​.

Observation operators in filterax¶

An operator in filterax maps one state vector to one observation vector. The analysis applies it across the members with jax.vmap(obs_op)(particles), so it never sees the ensemble axis.

Two forms are accepted, and they compute the same thing:

FormUse it whenExample
a plain callablehhh has no state of its ownlambda state: state[:3]
a subclass of flx.AbstractObsOperatorhhh carries arrays or trainable parametersa linear map, a learned network, flx.LiftedObs

AbstractObsOperator is an equinox.Module, and it requires one method, __call__(state). Being a pytree is what makes it useful. Its arrays are leaves, so eqx.filter_grad differentiates an analysis with respect to them, which is the mechanism chapter 14 trains with. flx.LiftedObs composes a decoder with an inner operator, so that a latent filter can observe through it (chapter 15).

It prints (30, 3) 0.0 and -3.999721 -3.999721, so the two forms agree exactly and the gradient matches a central difference.

A Layer 1 analysis takes obs_op per call, so each window can have its own operator. A Layer 2 model such as flx.ETKF holds one obs_op for the whole run. A moving observing network therefore needs a Layer 1 loop, as in chapter 1.

Most often the instrument noise is uncorrelated, and a lineax.DiagonalLinearOperator holds it. Correlated errors, such as spatially correlated satellite retrievals, can use a structured operator.

Perturbed observations¶

A stochastic filter updates each member against its own noisy copy of the data Burgers et al., 1998:

y(e)=y+ε(e),ε(e)=Lεξ(e),ξ(e)∼N(0,I),\vect{y}^{(e)} = \vect{y} + \gvect{\varepsilon}^{(e)}, \qquad \gvect{\varepsilon}^{(e)} = \mat{L}_\varepsilon \gvect{\xi}^{(e)}, \qquad \gvect{\xi}^{(e)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\vect{0}, \mat{I}),y(e)=y+ε(e),ε(e)=Lε​ξ(e),ξ(e)∼N(0,I),
(3.7)

which gives the analysis xt∣t(e)=x(e)+G^(y(e)−h(e))\vect{x}^{(e)}_{t \mid t} = \vect{x}^{(e)} + \hat{\mat{G}} (\vect{y}^{(e)} - \vect{h}^{(e)})xt∣t(e)​=x(e)+G^(y(e)−h(e)) with the gain G^∈RDx×Dy\hat{\mat{G}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_y}G^∈RDx​×Dy​ of chapter 4.

The perturbation is there for the spread. For a linear hhh, the analysis anomalies are (I−G^H)x~(e)+G^ε~(e)(\mat{I} - \hat{\mat{G}} \mat{H}) \tilde{\vect{x}}^{(e)} + \hat{\mat{G}} \tilde{\gvect{\varepsilon}}^{(e)}(I−G^H)x~(e)+G^ε~(e). With noise independent of the members, this gives in expectation

Σ^x∣y=(I−G^H)Σ^x(I−G^H)⊤+G^ΣεG^⊤,\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{x \mid y} = (\mat{I} - \hat{\mat{G}} \mat{H}) \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x (\mat{I} - \hat{\mat{G}} \mat{H})^\top + \hat{\mat{G}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon \hat{\mat{G}}^\top,Σ^x∣y​=(I−G^H)Σ^x​(I−G^H)⊤+G^Σε​G^⊤,
(3.8)

which is the correct Kalman covariance (chapter 4). Without the perturbations the last term is missing, and the ensemble ends up too narrow.

The draws have sampling noise of their own, and filterax does not recentre them. Their sample mean εˉ\bar{\gvect{\varepsilon}}εˉ has variance Σε/NE\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon / N_\mathcal{E}Σε​/NE​, and it moves the analysis mean by exactly G^εˉ\hat{\mat{G}} \bar{\gvect{\varepsilon}}G^εˉ:

xˉt∣t=xˉ+G^(rˉ+εˉ).\bar{\vect{x}}_{t \mid t} = \bar{\vect{x}} + \hat{\mat{G}} (\bar{\vect{r}} + \bar{\gvect{\varepsilon}}).xˉt∣t​=xˉ+G^(rˉ+εˉ).
(3.9)

When it breaks¶

A linearisation can be blind. With x∼N(0,1)x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)x∼N(0,1) and h(x)=x2h(x) = x^2h(x)=x2, the population covariance Σxy\gmat{\Sigma}_{xy}Σxy​ is zero, since xxx and x2x^2x2 are uncorrelated. The averaged Jacobian E[2x]\mathbb{E}[2 x]E[2x] is zero as well, so a value of yyy far from the prior should teach a lot and the update learns nothing. Program 3.6 sets y=4y = 4y=4 with noise variance 0.01, where the exact posterior has one mode near each of ±2\pm 2±2, and it computes that posterior by quadrature.

It prints -0.015 -0.067 1.027 1.031 and 0.000 3.994 3.994. The columns are the gain (first line only), then the mean, the variance and the mean of hhh. The exact posterior has variance near 4 and predicts the measurement, while the analysis keeps the prior’s unit variance and still predicts about 1. The small gain here comes from sampling alone.

Iterating does not rescue a blind linearisation, though it does help a poor one. Program 3.7 repeats the problem with EKI, and then with a one-sided prior N(1.5,0.04)\mathcal{N}(1.5, 0.04)N(1.5,0.04), where the exact posterior has a single mode.

It prints symmetric: 1.031 1.114 (50 steps, False) 3.994 and one-sided: 4.207 3.853 (11 steps, True) 3.968. The columns are the mean of hhh after the ETKF, after EKI and under the exact posterior. The symmetric prior stays near 1 either way, and EKI uses all its iterations without reaching the end of its schedule. With the one-sided prior, EKI halves the error of the single update.

A singular noise covariance fails on one route only. perturbed_observations handles a singular Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​, since a zero variance just gives a zero perturbation. The Woodbury route of the gain solves against Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​, so it returns NaN without raising, even when Σ^y\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yΣ^y​ is invertible. That is the default whenever NE<DyN_\mathcal{E} < D_yNE​<Dy​, and dense_innovation=True is the way out.

It prints [0. 0. 0. 0.], nan and 0.0919.

A repeated key repeats the noise. perturbed_observations consumes the key it is given, and the same key gives the same draws. StochasticEnKF.analysis falls back to its stored key when none is passed, so a hand-written loop has to pass a fresh key each window. The Layer 2 flx.StochasticEnKF folds a new key per window on its own.

It prints 10: 0.3088 0.3162, 100: 0.0988 0.1000, 1000: 0.0306 0.0316 and True, so the mean offset follows 1/NE1 / \sqrt{N_\mathcal{E}}1/NE​​ and the repeated key repeats every draw.

When each form is the right answer¶

Use a plain callable for a fixed hhh with nothing to train. Write an AbstractObsOperator when hhh has arrays you want to differentiate, or to compose it with other modules. Use a diagonal noise operator unless the errors really are correlated, and then keep the structure rather than forming a dense matrix. Use perturbed_observations directly only when you are writing a stochastic update by hand, since StochasticEnKF already draws them.

Think again about a Kalman update at all when hhh is strongly nonlinear across the ensemble, as the quadratic case showed. The iterated methods of chapter 9 help when the linearisation is merely poor, though not when it is blind.
