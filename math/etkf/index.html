ETKF: the ensemble transform Kalman filter

The stochastic EnKF (chapter 5) gets the analysis covariance right only in expectation, because every member sees its own perturbed observation and those draws add sampling noise to the update. The ETKF Bishop et al., 2001 removes the sampling step: it computes one deterministic transform of the forecast ensemble, whose sample mean and sample covariance match the Kalman analysis exactly.

Three ideas make it work, and they organise this chapter. The update can be written entirely in the NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​-dimensional ensemble space. Only the symmetric square root of the weight covariance keeps the ensemble centred on the Kalman mean. And the matrix being decomposed has a repeated eigenvalue and a huge dynamic range, so the way it is computed decides whether gradients and float32 survive.

The update in ensemble space¶

Take the forecast ensemble X∈RNE×Dx\mat{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_x}X∈RNE​×Dx​ with members as rows, its mean xˉ∈RDx\bar{\vect{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x}xˉ∈RDx​ and its anomalies X~=X−1xˉ⊤\tilde{\mat{X}} = \mat{X} - \vect{1} \bar{\vect{x}}^\topX~=X−1xˉ⊤. Map each member through the observation operator, h(e)=h(x(e))\vect{h}^{(e)} = h(\vect{x}^{(e)})h(e)=h(x(e)), to get the mean hˉ∈RDy\bar{\vect{h}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}hˉ∈RDy​, the anomalies H~∈RNE×Dy\tilde{\mat{H}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_y}H~∈RNE​×Dy​ and the mean residual rˉ=y−hˉ\bar{\vect{r}} = \vect{y} - \bar{\vect{h}}rˉ=y−hˉ. The sample covariances are then Σ^xy=X~⊤H~/c\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy} = \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}} / cΣ^xy​=X~⊤H~/c and Σ^h=H~⊤H~/c\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h = \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}} / cΣ^h​=H~⊤H~/c, where c=NE−1c = N_\mathcal{E} - 1c=NE​−1.

The Kalman update moves the state only within the span of the forecast anomalies. So rather than work with Dx×DxD_x \times D_xDx​×Dx​ covariances, write the analysis as a recombination of the forecast members and solve for the weights. Define the precision of the weights in ensemble space,

Λw=cI+H~Σε−1H~⊤,Σw=Λw−1,\gmat{\Lambda}_w = c \mat{I} + \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top, \qquad \gmat{\Sigma}_w = \gmat{\Lambda}_w^{-1},Λw​=cI+H~Σε−1​H~⊤,Σw​=Λw−1​,
(6.1)

both NE×NEN_\mathcal{E} \times N_\mathcal{E}NE​×NE​. The mean weights wˉ∈RNE\bar{\vect{w}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E}}wˉ∈RNE​ and the transform W∈RNE×NE\mat{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times N_\mathcal{E}}W∈RNE​×NE​ are

wˉ=ΣwH~Σε−1rˉ,W=(cΣw)1/2,\bar{\vect{w}} = \gmat{\Sigma}_w \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \bar{\vect{r}}, \qquad \mat{W} = (c \gmat{\Sigma}_w)^{1/2},wˉ=Σw​H~Σε−1​rˉ,W=(cΣw​)1/2,
(6.2)

and the analysis ensemble is assembled from the forecast anomalies alone:

xˉt∣t=xˉ+X~⊤wˉ,X~t∣t=WX~.\bar{\vect{x}}_{t \mid t} = \bar{\vect{x}} + \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \bar{\vect{w}}, \qquad \tilde{\mat{X}}_{t \mid t} = \mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}}.xˉt∣t​=xˉ+X~⊤wˉ,X~t∣t​=WX~.
(6.3)

Why this is the Kalman update¶

The gain follows from a push-through identity. For Σ^y=Σ^h+Σε\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_y = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h + \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣ^y​=Σ^h​+Σε​,

G^=Σ^xyΣ^y−1=X~⊤ΣwH~Σε−1,\hat{\mat{G}} = \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_y^{-1} = \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \gmat{\Sigma}_w \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1},G^=Σ^xy​Σ^y−1​=X~⊤Σw​H~Σε−1​,
(6.4)

which can be checked by multiplying H~Σ^y−1/c=ΣwH~Σε−1\tilde{\mat{H}} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_y^{-1} / c = \gmat{\Sigma}_w \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1}H~Σ^y−1​/c=Σw​H~Σε−1​ by Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ on the left and Σ^y\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yΣ^y​ on the right, where both sides become H~Σε−1Σ^y\tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yH~Σε−1​Σ^y​. So xˉ+G^rˉ\bar{\vect{x}} + \hat{\mat{G}} \bar{\vect{r}}xˉ+G^rˉ is the mean in (6.3).

The covariance follows in the same way. The Kalman analysis covariance is Σ^x−G^Σ^xy⊤\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x - \hat{\mat{G}} \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy}^\topΣ^x​−G^Σ^xy⊤​, and substituting (6.4) gives

Σ^x∣y=1cX~⊤(I−ΣwH~Σε−1H~⊤)X~=X~⊤ΣwX~,\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{x \mid y} = \frac{1}{c} \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \left(\mat{I} - \gmat{\Sigma}_w \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top\right) \tilde{\mat{X}} = \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \gmat{\Sigma}_w \tilde{\mat{X}},Σ^x∣y​=c1​X~⊤(I−Σw​H~Σε−1​H~⊤)X~=X~⊤Σw​X~,
(6.5)

since H~Σε−1H~⊤=Λw−cI\tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top = \gmat{\Lambda}_w - c \mat{I}H~Σε−1​H~⊤=Λw​−cI. The anomalies WX~\mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}}WX~ have sample covariance X~⊤W⊤WX~/c\tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \mat{W}^\top \mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}} / cX~⊤W⊤WX~/c, so any W\mat{W}W with W⊤W=cΣw\mat{W}^\top \mat{W} = c \gmat{\Sigma}_wW⊤W=cΣw​ reproduces (6.5) exactly, with no randomness anywhere. For a nonlinear hhh the same algebra holds with the sample covariances, which is a statistical linearisation of hhh over the ensemble and not the exact posterior.

Why the symmetric square root¶

The condition W⊤W=cΣw\mat{W}^\top \mat{W} = c \gmat{\Sigma}_wW⊤W=cΣw​ leaves a rotation free, and not every choice gives a valid ensemble. The analysis anomalies must stay centred, 1⊤X~t∣t=0\vect{1}^\top \tilde{\mat{X}}_{t \mid t} = \vect{0}1⊤X~t∣t​=0, otherwise the sample mean of the analysis drifts away from the Kalman mean and the filter is biased Livings et al., 2008.

Anomalies sum to zero, H~⊤1=0\tilde{\mat{H}}^\top \vect{1} = \vect{0}H~⊤1=0, so the constant vector is an eigenvector of (6.1), with Λw1=c1\gmat{\Lambda}_w \vect{1} = c \vect{1}Λw​1=c1. The symmetric square root keeps that eigenvector, and its eigenvalue is one:

W1=(cΣw)1/21=1.\mat{W} \vect{1} = (c \gmat{\Sigma}_w)^{1/2} \vect{1} = \vect{1}.W1=(cΣw​)1/21=1.
(6.6)

Then 1⊤WX~=(W1)⊤X~=1⊤X~=0\vect{1}^\top \mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}} = (\mat{W} \vect{1})^\top \tilde{\mat{X}} = \vect{1}^\top \tilde{\mat{X}} = \vect{0}1⊤WX~=(W1)⊤X~=1⊤X~=0, so the symmetric root maps centred anomalies to centred anomalies. A Cholesky factor has no such property, and neither does a general W\mat{W}W Tippett et al., 2003Wang et al., 2004.

Breaking the symmetry: ETKF_Livings¶

The symmetric transform is deterministic, and over many cycles it can leave the ensemble with a few outlying members and non-Gaussian higher moments. The rotation that remains free can be spent on this. Compose the transform with a random rotation Q∈RNE×NE\mat{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times N_\mathcal{E}}Q∈RNE​×NE​ that fixes the constant vector,

X~t∣t=QWX~,Q⊤Q=I,Q1=1.\tilde{\mat{X}}_{t \mid t} = \mat{Q} \mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}}, \qquad \mat{Q}^\top \mat{Q} = \mat{I}, \qquad \mat{Q} \vect{1} = \vect{1}.X~t∣t​=QWX~,Q⊤Q=I,Q1=1.
(6.7)

Applied on the left, Q\mat{Q}Q leaves the sample covariance alone, because (QW)⊤QW=W⊤W(\mat{Q} \mat{W})^\top \mat{Q} \mat{W} = \mat{W}^\top \mat{W}(QW)⊤QW=W⊤W, and Q⊤1=1\mat{Q}^\top \vect{1} = \vect{1}Q⊤1=1 keeps the anomalies centred. ETKF_Livings draws it uniformly in the (NE−1)(N_\mathcal{E} - 1)(NE​−1)-dimensional complement of 1\vect{1}1, from the QR factor of a Gaussian matrix lifted back with a Householder basis. Use a fresh key every cycle, since repeating one rotation defeats the purpose.

Few observations: the rank-DyD_yDy​ route¶

The excess of Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ over cIc \mat{I}cI has rank at most DyD_yDy​. So when Dy<NED_y < N_\mathcal{E}Dy​<NE​ there is no need for an NE×NEN_\mathcal{E} \times N_\mathcal{E}NE​×NE​ decomposition: write

H~Σε−1H~⊤=Udiag(λ)U⊤,\tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top = \mat{U} \op{diag}(\gvect{\lambda}) \mat{U}^\top,H~Σε−1​H~⊤=Udiag(λ)U⊤,
(6.8)

with U∈RNE×R\mat{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times R}U∈RNE​×R orthonormal, λ∈RR\gvect{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^Rλ∈RR non-negative and R≤DyR \le D_yR≤Dy​. A thin QR of H~\tilde{\mat{H}}H~ gives a basis, and a Dy×DyD_y \times D_yDy​×Dy​ eigendecomposition finishes the job. Every eigenvalue on the complement of U\mat{U}U equals ccc, so any function of Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ needs only these factors:

ϕ(Λw)=ϕ(c)I+Udiag(ϕ(c+λ)−ϕ(c))U⊤.\phi(\gmat{\Lambda}_w) = \phi(c) \mat{I} + \mat{U} \op{diag}\big(\phi(c + \gvect{\lambda}) - \phi(c)\big) \mat{U}^\top.ϕ(Λw​)=ϕ(c)I+Udiag(ϕ(c+λ)−ϕ(c))U⊤.
(6.9)

With ϕ(γ)=1/γ\phi(\gamma) = 1 / \gammaϕ(γ)=1/γ this applies Σw\gmat{\Sigma}_wΣw​, and with ϕ(γ)=γ−1/2\phi(\gamma) = \gamma^{-1/2}ϕ(γ)=γ−1/2 it applies the root in X~t∣t=cΛw−1/2X~\tilde{\mat{X}}_{t \mid t} = \sqrt{c} \gmat{\Lambda}_w^{-1/2} \tilde{\mat{X}}X~t∣t​=c​Λw−1/2​X~. Both are matrix-vector products, so no NE×NEN_\mathcal{E} \times N_\mathcal{E}NE​×NE​ array exists on this route. gaussx.etkf_analysis takes it by default, and filters.ETKF calls that function.

Implementation in filterax¶

filterax.filters.ETKF is the Layer 1 analysis step: one analysis ensemble from one observation vector. The example has NE=30N_\mathcal{E} = 30NE​=30 members of a state with Dx=4D_x = 4Dx​=4, drawn around 1 with standard deviation 0.5, and observes components 0 and 2 with noise variance 0.1.

It prints [0.43 0.528 0.531 0.522] [0.255 0.516 0.272 0.511] and -3.401. The observed components contract and the others move only through their sample correlation with them, which is why components 1 and 3 change a little.

result.log_likelihood is the Gaussian evidence of the residual under Σ^y\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yΣ^y​, which chapter 13 uses for diagnostics. The Layer 2 filterax.ETKF wraps this step with dynamics, inflation and the multi-window assimilate loop of chapter 16. A rotated analysis comes from filters.ETKF_Livings in the same way.

It prints True, True and 1.86: the same mean and covariance, with members that have moved by up to 1.86.

Differentiating the transform¶

The ensemble-space route eigendecomposes Λw=Φdiag(γ)Φ⊤\gmat{\Lambda}_w = \gmat{\Phi} \op{diag}(\gvect{\gamma}) \gmat{\Phi}^\topΛw​=Φdiag(γ)Φ⊤, and by (6.8) the eigenvalue ccc is repeated NE−rank(H~)N_\mathcal{E} - \op{rank}(\tilde{\mat{H}})NE​−rank(H~) times, which is at least twice whenever Dy≤NE−2D_y \le N_\mathcal{E} - 2Dy​≤NE​−2. JAX’s rule for eigh divides by the gaps γi−γj\gamma_i - \gamma_jγi​−γj​, so differentiating through it gives a NaN gradient at exactly the matrices the filter always produces, and any training loop (chapter 14) would fail on its first step.

The derivative of a spectral function needs no gaps once it is written in closed form. For ϕ(Λw)=Φϕ(diag(γ))Φ⊤\phi(\gmat{\Lambda}_w) = \gmat{\Phi} \phi(\op{diag}(\gvect{\gamma})) \gmat{\Phi}^\topϕ(Λw​)=Φϕ(diag(γ))Φ⊤ the Daleckii-Krein formula gives

dϕ(Λw)=Φ(D∘Φ⊤dΛwΦ)Φ⊤,[D]ij=ϕ(γi)−ϕ(γj)γi−γj,\mathrm{d} \phi(\gmat{\Lambda}_w) = \gmat{\Phi} \left(\mat{D} \circ \gmat{\Phi}^\top \mathrm{d} \gmat{\Lambda}_w \gmat{\Phi}\right) \gmat{\Phi}^\top, \qquad [\mat{D}]_{ij} = \frac{\phi(\gamma_i) - \phi(\gamma_j)}{\gamma_i - \gamma_j},dϕ(Λw​)=Φ(D∘Φ⊤dΛw​Φ)Φ⊤,[D]ij​=γi​−γj​ϕ(γi​)−ϕ(γj​)​,
(6.10)

where ∘ is the element-wise product and [D]ii=ϕ′(γi)[\mat{D}]_{ii} = \phi'(\gamma_i)[D]ii​=ϕ′(γi​). For the two functions needed here the difference cancels algebraically:

γi−1−γj−1γi−γj=−1γiγj,γi−1/2−γj−1/2γi−γj=−1γiγj (γi+γj),\frac{\gamma_i^{-1} - \gamma_j^{-1}}{\gamma_i - \gamma_j} = \frac{-1}{\gamma_i \gamma_j}, \qquad \frac{\gamma_i^{-1/2} - \gamma_j^{-1/2}}{\gamma_i - \gamma_j} = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{\gamma_i \gamma_j} \, (\sqrt{\gamma_i} + \sqrt{\gamma_j})},γi​−γj​γi−1​−γj−1​​=γi​γj​−1​,γi​−γj​γi−1/2​−γj−1/2​​=γi​γj​​(γi​​+γj​​)−1​,
(6.11)

and both are finite however many eigenvalues coincide. gaussx installs this as a custom derivative, and the rank-DyD_yDy​ route applies the same rule in blocks on U\mat{U}U and its complement.

Float32 and strong observations¶

The eigenvalues of Λw\gmat{\Lambda}_wΛw​ run from ccc up to about ∥H~Σε−1H~⊤∥\Vert \tilde{\mat{H}} \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^\top \Vert∥H~Σε−1​H~⊤∥, and an eigendecomposition resolves them only to about machine epsilon ϵ\epsilonϵ times the largest. In float32, with ϵ≈10−7\epsilon \approx 10^{-7}ϵ≈10−7, a strong observation therefore loses the eigenvalues near ccc altogether, and they can even come out negative. Two things follow.

The bound γi≥c\gamma_i \ge cγi​≥c is exact, so the ensemble-space route floors the computed eigenvalues at ccc before taking roots. Without the floor, a rounded negative eigenvalue gives a non-finite transform.

The mean update cancels on the rank-DyD_yDy​ route. With the whitened residual v=Σε−1rˉ∈RDy\vect{v} = \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon^{-1} \bar{\vect{r}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y}v=Σε−1​rˉ∈RDy​, reading U⊤H~v\mat{U}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}} \vect{v}U⊤H~v directly lets a weak row of U⊤H~\mat{U}^\top \tilde{\mat{H}}U⊤H~ carry rounding from a strong mode into the large part of v\vect{v}v. So gaussx reads the strong modes first and removes each from v\vect{v}v by an oblique projection, which leaves the weak modes a well-scaled remainder.

Program 6.6 measures the result against a float64 dense solve, with noise variances from 10-6 to 106.

When it breaks¶

The transform is exact for the sample covariance, so it inherits every weakness of the sample covariance. The anomalies of WX~\mat{W} \tilde{\mat{X}}WX~ span no more than those of X~\tilde{\mat{X}}X~, so the update cannot move the state outside the forecast’s NE−1N_\mathcal{E} - 1NE​−1 directions. A small ensemble then underestimates its own error and trusts the forecast too much, the observations stop mattering, and the spread collapses while the error grows.

Program 6.7 shows this on Lorenz-96 with Dx=40D_x = 40Dx​=40, half the sites observed with unit variance, and no inflation or localization.

It prints N_E = 20: RMSE 2.37, spread 0.26 and N_E = 40: RMSE 0.31, spread 0.25. With 40 members the spread matches the error, which is a healthy filter, while with 20 the error is nine times the spread and is worse than the unit observation noise. The treatments are localization (chapter 8, chapter 11), which stops distant sites competing for those few directions, and inflation (chapter 12), which restores the lost spread.

Three other limits are worth knowing:

  • A strongly nonlinear hhh makes (6.3) a linearisation over the ensemble, and an iterated method (chapter 10) does better.

  • A non-Gaussian prior biases the update, and the bias does not shrink with ensemble size.

  • The symmetric transform can leave a few outlying members over long runs, which is what ETKF_Livings addresses.

When ETKF is the right answer¶

Use filters.ETKF when you want a deterministic, reproducible analysis, since no perturbation noise enters. It suits small and moderate ensembles, where the O(1/NE)O(1 / \sqrt{N_\mathcal{E}})O(1/NE​​) sampling error of the stochastic EnKF is the dominant error. It also suits differentiable pipelines, and it is the inner solver of LETKF, which runs one ETKF per grid point.

Reach for something else when the state is much larger than the ensemble with no localization (use LETKF), or when you want to process observations one at a time, which is EnSRF_Serial in chapter 7.
