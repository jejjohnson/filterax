Localization

A finite ensemble estimates a Dx×DxD_x \times D_xDx​×Dx​ covariance from NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​ samples, and the entries between unrelated variables come out random instead of zero. The gain takes those entries at face value, so every observation moves the whole state a little, in directions that are pure sampling noise. Localization damps covariance entries as a function of the physical distance between variables. The prior knowledge behind it is that real correlations decay with distance and sampling noise does not.

Three questions organise this chapter. Why does an element-wise product of a covariance with a taper remain a covariance? What does a radius mean, given that the tapers in filterax read it differently? And where in the library is a taper applied, given that the covariance form and the observation-weighting form live in different layers?

Sampling noise in the covariance¶

Take the state ensemble X∈RNE×Dx\mat{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\mathcal{E} \times D_x}X∈RNE​×Dx​ with anomalies X~\tilde{\mat{X}}X~, and the sample covariance Σ^x=X~⊤X~/(NE−1)\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x = \tilde{\mat{X}}^\top \tilde{\mat{X}} / (N_\mathcal{E} - 1)Σ^x​=X~⊤X~/(NE​−1). Its rank is at most NE−1N_\mathcal{E} - 1NE​−1, so it has no information outside that many directions. Its sample correlations, for components iii and jjj with spreads σi\sigma_iσi​ and σj\sigma_jσj​, are

c^ij=[Σ^x]ijσiσj,\hat{c}_{ij} = \frac{[\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x]_{ij}}{\sigma_i \sigma_j},c^ij​=σi​σj​[Σ^x​]ij​​,
(11.1)

and when the true correlation is zero their standard deviation is about 1/NE−11 / \sqrt{N_\mathcal{E} - 1}1/NE​−1​. That size depends on neither the distance between the variables nor the state dimension.

It prints 0.229 0.229 and 0.81. The measured scatter matches 1/191 / \sqrt{19}1/19​, and the largest of these false correlations is 0.81.

The Schur product¶

The localized covariance is the Schur, or element-wise, product of the sample covariance with a taper matrix ρxx∈RDx×Dx\rho_{xx} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_x}ρxx​∈RDx​×Dx​:

Σ^xloc=ρxx∘Σ^x,[ρxx]ij=ρ(dij;ℓ),\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x^\text{loc} = \rho_{xx} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x, \qquad [\rho_{xx}]_{ij} = \rho(d_{ij}; \ell),Σ^xloc​=ρxx​∘Σ^x​,[ρxx​]ij​=ρ(dij​;ℓ),
(11.2)

where dijd_{ij}dij​ is the distance between variables iii and jjj and ℓ\ellℓ is the taper’s half-width, the radius argument.

The Schur product theorem¶

If two square matrices are positive semi-definite, so is their Schur product. The proof fits on a line. Write each matrix as a sum of outer products, ρxx=∑kakak⊤\rho_{xx} = \sum_k \vect{a}_k \vect{a}_k^\topρxx​=∑k​ak​ak⊤​ and Σ^x=∑lblbl⊤\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x = \sum_l \vect{b}_l \vect{b}_l^\topΣ^x​=∑l​bl​bl⊤​, and multiply element by element:

ρxx∘Σ^x=∑k,l(ak∘bl)(ak∘bl)⊤,\rho_{xx} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x = \sum_{k, l} (\vect{a}_k \circ \vect{b}_l)(\vect{a}_k \circ \vect{b}_l)^\top,ρxx​∘Σ^x​=k,l∑​(ak​∘bl​)(ak​∘bl​)⊤,
(11.3)

which is a sum of outer products, hence positive semi-definite. So a taper that is itself a valid correlation function can never break the covariance, however hard it cuts.

(11.3) also shows two more things. The rank can grow to the product of the two ranks, so localization adds directions the ensemble did not span. And with ρ(0;ℓ)=1\rho(0; \ell) = 1ρ(0;ℓ)=1 the diagonal is untouched, so localization keeps every variance and changes only the correlations.

The tapers¶

Each taper below takes distances and a radius ℓ\ellℓ, and returns weights with the shape of the distances. With z=d/ℓz = d / \ellz=d/ℓ, the Gaspari-Cohn taper Gaspari & Cohn, 1999 is a fifth-order piecewise rational function,

ρGC(z)={−14z5+12z4+58z3−53z2+10≤z≤1,112z5−12z4+58z3+53z2−5z+4−23z1<z≤2,0z>2.\rho_\text{GC}(z) = \begin{cases} -\frac{1}{4} z^5 + \frac{1}{2} z^4 + \frac{5}{8} z^3 - \frac{5}{3} z^2 + 1 & 0 \le z \le 1, \\ \frac{1}{12} z^5 - \frac{1}{2} z^4 + \frac{5}{8} z^3 + \frac{5}{3} z^2 - 5 z + 4 - \frac{2}{3 z} & 1 < z \le 2, \\ 0 & z > 2. \end{cases}ρGC​(z)=⎩⎨⎧​−41​z5+21​z4+85​z3−35​z2+1121​z5−21​z4+85​z3+35​z2−5z+4−3z2​0​0≤z≤1,1<z≤2,z>2.​
(11.4)

It vanishes at d=2ℓd = 2 \elld=2ℓ, which is why filterax calls ℓ\ellℓ a half-width. Its construction by convolution makes it a correlation function in up to three dimensions, which is what the theorem needs. The other three are

ρGauss(d)=exp⁡(−d22ℓ2),ρSOAR(d)=(1+dℓ)exp⁡(−dℓ),ρcut(d)=1{d≤ℓ}.\rho_\text{Gauss}(d) = \exp\left(-\frac{d^2}{2 \ell^2}\right), \qquad \rho_\text{SOAR}(d) = \left(1 + \frac{d}{\ell}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{d}{\ell}\right), \qquad \rho_\text{cut}(d) = \mathbb{1}\{d \le \ell\}.ρGauss​(d)=exp(−2ℓ2d2​),ρSOAR​(d)=(1+ℓd​)exp(−ℓd​),ρcut​(d)=1{d≤ℓ}.
(11.5)

The Gaussian has no compact support, and neither does the SOAR taper Thiébaux & Pedder, 1987, which is a Matérn-3/23/23/2 correlation with length scale 3ℓ\sqrt{3} \ell3​ℓ. Both are correlation functions in any dimension.

The hard cutoff is not. In one dimension its Fourier transform is 2sin⁡(ωℓ)/ω2 \sin(\omega \ell) / \omega2sin(ωℓ)/ω, which changes sign. So by Bochner’s theorem it is not positive definite, and the Schur product theorem does not apply to it.

Distances and the taper matrix¶

filterax.localization_matrix(coords_a, coords_b, radius, metric=...) builds ρ(d(si,sj);ℓ)\rho(d(\vect{s}_i, \vect{s}_j); \ell)ρ(d(si​,sj​);ℓ) for two sets of coordinates, and always with the Gaspari-Cohn taper. It calls gaussx.localization_matrix with compact-support argument c = 2 * radius, since gaussx parametrises by support and filterax by half-width. The default metric is euclidean_distance. haversine_distance takes (latitude,longitude)(\text{latitude}, \text{longitude})(latitude,longitude) in radians and returns metres on a sphere of radius 6.371×1066.371 \times 10^66.371×106 m by default, so ℓ\ellℓ must then be in metres too. A metric is any function (coords_a, coords_b) -> distances.

A periodic domain needs its own metric, and with it the theorem has a limit. The arc distance on a circle is not a Euclidean distance, so ρGC\rho_\text{GC}ρGC​ of it need not be positive definite. Program 11.4 measures this.

The smallest eigenvalue on the circle of circumference 40 is 0.0139 for ℓ=2\ell = 2ℓ=2, 0.0003 for ℓ=8\ell = 8ℓ=8 and -0.0023 for ℓ=12\ell = 12ℓ=12. So the periodic taper is safe while the support 2ℓ2 \ell2ℓ stays within half the domain, and it turned indefinite at 2ℓ=242 \ell = 242ℓ=24. One degree of longitude prints as 111195 metres. On 200 uniform points on the sphere, with great-circle distance and ℓ=1\ell = 1ℓ=1, the smallest eigenvalue is 1.4e-05. That is positive on this draw and proves nothing in general.

The localized gain¶

Using (11.2) as the forecast covariance in the Kalman update of chapter 4, with a linear hhh that selects observed components, gives

Gloc=(ρxx∘Σ^x)H⊤(H(ρxx∘Σ^x)H⊤+Σε)−1.\mat{G}_\text{loc} = (\rho_{xx} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x) \mat{H}^\top \left(\mat{H} (\rho_{xx} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_x) \mat{H}^\top + \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon\right)^{-1}.Gloc​=(ρxx​∘Σ^x​)H⊤(H(ρxx​∘Σ^x​)H⊤+Σε​)−1.
(11.7)

Selecting rows and columns of an element-wise product is the same as multiplying the selected rows and columns. So with the state-observation taper ρxy∈RDx×Dy\rho_{xy} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_y}ρxy​∈RDx​×Dy​ and the observation-observation taper ρyy∈RDy×Dy\rho_{yy} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_y}ρyy​∈RDy​×Dy​ this is

Gloc=(ρxy∘Σ^xy)(ρyy∘Σ^h+Σε)−1,\mat{G}_\text{loc} = (\rho_{xy} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy}) (\rho_{yy} \circ \hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_h + \gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilon)^{-1},Gloc​=(ρxy​∘Σ^xy​)(ρyy​∘Σ^h​+Σε​)−1,
(11.8)

with Gloc∈RDx×Dy\mat{G}_\text{loc} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x \times D_y}Gloc​∈RDx​×Dy​. This is covariance localization, also called B-localization Houtekamer & Mitchell, 2001Hamill et al., 2001. It is localized_kalman_gain, and (11.8) is also what it computes for a nonlinear hhh or one that averages over a footprint. For those, (11.8) needs positions for the observations and is no longer the same as (11.7) Campbell et al., 2010.

There is a real cost. Without tapers, Σ^y\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yΣ^y​ is a rank-(NE−1)(N_\mathcal{E} - 1)(NE​−1) update of Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​ with structured solves. The Schur product destroys that structure, so localized_kalman_gain forms the dense Dy×DyD_y \times D_yDy​×Dy​ matrix and pays O(NEDxDy+NEDy2+Dy3+DxDy2)O(N_\mathcal{E} D_x D_y + N_\mathcal{E} D_y^2 + D_y^3 + D_x D_y^2)O(NE​Dx​Dy​+NE​Dy2​+Dy3​+Dx​Dy2​). That is the algebraic reason LETKF (chapter 8) works with observation weights instead, as the implementation section sets out.

Program 11.5 checks the production gain against (11.7) in dense numpy, and checks three further facts: the unit taper, gain tapering and a footprint operator.

It prints 3e-15, 4e-16, 0.33 0.72 and 0.26 0.27. The production gain matches the dense form and the unit taper recovers kalman_gain, both to float64 round-off. Adaptive localization¶

A distance-based taper needs a radius, and it needs distances. adaptive_localization needs neither: it builds a Dx×DyD_x \times D_yDx​×Dy​ mask from the ensemble, keeping an entry only when its sample correlation exceeds its own approximate standard error. With c^ij\hat{c}_{ij}c^ij​ now the correlation between xix_ixi​ and hjh_jhj​, and τ\tauτ the significance argument,

[ρxy]ij=1{∣c^ij∣>τ1−c^ij2NE−2}.[\rho_{xy}]_{ij} = \mathbb{1}\left\{|\hat{c}_{ij}| > \tau \frac{1 - \hat{c}_{ij}^2}{\sqrt{N_\mathcal{E} - 2}}\right\}.[ρxy​]ij​=1{∣c^ij​∣>τNE​−2​1−c^ij2​​}.
(11.9)

The motivation comes from the hierarchical filter of Anderson (2007), which asks when a regression coefficient is too noisy to trust. The code does not run a hierarchy of ensembles: (11.9) is one hard threshold on one ensemble.

Solving the boundary of (11.9) for ∣c^∣|\hat{c}|∣c^∣ gives the threshold in closed form,

c⋆=NE−2(1+4τ2/(NE−2)−1)2τ,c^\star = \frac{\sqrt{N_\mathcal{E} - 2} \left(\sqrt{1 + 4 \tau^2 / (N_\mathcal{E} - 2)} - 1\right)}{2 \tau},c⋆=2τNE​−2​(1+4τ2/(NE​−2)​−1)​,
(11.10)

so τ\tauτ is roughly a z-score and the keep rate of a null correlation is set by τ\tauτ, not by NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​.

The default τ=1\tau = 1τ=1 keeps about a third of the pure-noise entries at any ensemble size, and τ=3\tau = 3τ=3 keeps about 2% of them. So the mask thins the noise, with a threshold that you still have to choose. It is not positive definite, and it raises ValueError below NE=3N_\mathcal{E} = 3NE​=3. Its docstring applies it to the finished gain, with localize, and the cycle below shows why that matters.

LETKF has its own cut¶

LETKF weights each observation’s inverse variance by ρ\rhoρ at each grid point, which is R-localization Hunt et al., 2007 (chapter 8), and that keeps the local solves small. It computes its own Euclidean distance, so it takes no metric, and on a sphere the coordinates must be Cartesian. It also drops every observation beyond d=ℓd = \elld=ℓ before tapering, so the second branch of (11.4) is never used. The result is a support of ℓ\ellℓ, half that of localization_matrix at the same radius, with a jump at the cut.

It prints [4.65 0.365 0. ] [1. 0.6849 0.0165]. The site at d=3d = 3d=3 has a Gaspari-Cohn weight of 0.0165 from localization_matrix, and it receives no increment at all from LETKF. The jump at the cut is 0.2083, the value of (11.4) at z=1z = 1z=1.

Gradients and float32¶

The tapers are differentiable in ℓ\ellℓ through localization_matrix, so a half-width can be trained (chapter 14). Gaussx guards the 1/z1 / z1/z term, so the gradient at d=0d = 0d=0 is finite. hard_cutoff and adaptive_localization are comparisons, so their gradients are zero everywhere and no error warns you.

It prints 0.147164 0.147164, 0.0, 0.0 and 0.0, and then float32 -1.2e-06 643. So the gradient matches the central difference, and the gradients of the two comparisons are exactly zero. The last line is the one place float32 shows. The rational branch of (11.4) cancels large terms near z=2z = 2z=2, so 643 of those 20,001 float32 values come out slightly negative, as low as −1.2×10−6-1.2 \times 10^{-6}−1.2×10−6. That is round-off in a weight meant to be in [0,1][0, 1][0,1], and it is far smaller than any entry that matters.

When it breaks¶

The table above has two nan rows, and Program 11.10 shows why the hard cutoff gives one.

It prints hard_cutoff -0.27 and gaspari_cohn 2.10. With the hard cutoff, the matrix that localized_kalman_gain tags as positive semi-definite has a negative eigenvalue in the first window. The run then returns nan. The Gaspari-Cohn taper with the same support stays above the noise variance of 1.

The limits, in the order you are likely to meet them:

  • A hard cutoff can kill the filter, as above, though at other settings in the table it survives, so keep it as a debugging baseline only.

  • Too small a half-width cuts real signal and leaves the state underconstrained, as the ℓ=1\ell = 1ℓ=1 row shows.

  • Too large a half-width lets noise back in, which is the ℓ=8\ell = 8ℓ=8 row at NE=10N_\mathcal{E} = 10NE​=10.

  • A data-driven mask needs its own tuning: in the table it works only at NE=20N_\mathcal{E} = 20NE​=20 with τ=2\tau = 2τ=2 on the finished gain.

  • The same mask on Σ^xy\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_{xy}Σ^xy​ alone, with Σ^y\hat{\gmat{\Sigma}}_yΣ^y​ left untapered, returns nan.

  • The half-width has no universal value, since it depends on NEN_\mathcal{E}NE​, on the observation density and on the system.

  • Tapers damp correlations by distance whatever their physical origin, so relations such as balance between wind and mass fields are weakened Kepert, 2009.

  • A satellite radiance or a column average has no clear position, and (11.8) then differs from (11.7), as Program 11.5 measures.

  • A periodic metric can leave the theorem behind, so keep the support below half the domain, as Program 11.4 measures.

When localization is the right answer¶

Use a taper when NE≪DxN_\mathcal{E} \ll D_xNE​≪Dx​ and correlations decay with a distance you can compute, which covers most geophysical grids. Use LETKF for a large state with diagonal observation noise, where the local solves are small and parallel. Use localized_kalman_gain when you need a global gain, correlated observations through ρyy\rho_{yy}ρyy​, or a taper in ℓ\ellℓ you want to differentiate. Use adaptive_localization when no meaningful distance exists, and tune τ\tauτ rather than taking the default, as the table above shows. For a parameter vector with no spatial order, ledoit_wolf_shrinkage in chapter 12 is the alternative.
